<p>Cats are masters of concealment, and hiding is one of their most reliable ways of telling you something is wrong — the challenge is figuring out whether it's emotional or medical.</p>

<h2>Situational Hiding</h2>
<p>New visitors, loud noises, a new pet in the home, or a change in routine can all cause temporary hiding that resolves once the trigger passes and your cat feels secure again.</p>

<h2>Medical Hiding</h2>
<p>Pain, nausea, and illness are common medical reasons cats withdraw and hide — this is an instinctive behavior rooted in not appearing vulnerable when unwell.</p>

<h2>How to Tell the Difference</h2>
<p>Situational hiding usually resolves within a day or so and the cat returns to normal behavior. Hiding that persists, or is combined with reduced appetite, changes in litter box habits, or reduced grooming, points toward a medical cause.</p>

<h2>When to Act</h2>
<p>Persistent hiding beyond a day or two, especially with any other symptom change, warrants a veterinary visit — don't assume it's "just personality" without ruling out pain or illness first.</p>

<h2>Frequently Asked Questions</h2>
<p><strong>My cat always hides when guests come over — is that normal?</strong><br>Yes, this is common situational behavior and usually resolves once guests leave.</p>
<p><strong>Can hiding alone (with no other symptoms) still mean illness?</strong><br>It can, especially if it's a new pattern for your specific cat — cats hide pain very effectively, so a behavior change alone is worth noting.</p>
<p><strong>Does age affect how cats respond to stress?</strong><br>Yes — older cats and cats with chronic conditions may hide more readily when unwell, making sudden changes more significant to track.</p>
<p><strong>Should I try to coax my cat out of hiding?</strong><br>Gently, without force — but if it's persistent, focus on getting a veterinary evaluation rather than just managing the behavior.</p>
