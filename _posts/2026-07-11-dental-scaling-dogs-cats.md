<h2>Why Dental Health Matters</h2>
<p>Dental disease is not just about bad breath. When bacteria accumulate in the mouth and form tartar (hardened calculus), they can enter the bloodstream and cause:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Heart valve disease</strong> — bacteria settling on heart valves</li>
<li><strong>Kidney damage</strong> — chronic low-grade infection taxes kidney filtration</li>
<li><strong>Liver disease</strong> — bacterial toxins processed by the liver</li>
<li><strong>Chronic pain</strong> — loose, infected teeth cause constant discomfort that pets hide</li>
</ul>
<p>Dogs and cats do not show pain the way humans do. A pet with severe dental disease often continues eating — right up until they cannot.</p>

<div class="post-highlight">🔍 Clinical insight: At Shivraj Pet Clinic, we frequently see pet parents surprised at how much brighter and more energetic their pet becomes after a long-overdue dental scaling — chronic oral pain is often mistaken for "just getting older."</div>

<h2>Signs Your Pet Needs Dental Attention</h2>
<ul>
<li>Bad breath (beyond normal "dog breath")</li>
<li>Yellow or brown deposits on teeth near the gum line</li>
<li>Red, swollen, or bleeding gums</li>
<li>Loose teeth</li>
<li>Drooling more than usual</li>
<li>Pawing at the mouth</li>
<li>Preference for soft food or dropping food while eating</li>
<li>Facial swelling under the eye (often indicates a tooth root abscess)</li>
</ul>

<h2>Myth vs Fact</h2>
<table>
<tr><th>Myth</th><th>Fact</th></tr>
<tr><td class="myth-cell">Anaesthesia-free dentistry is a safer alternative</td><td class="fact-cell">A conscious pet cannot hold still for sub-gingival (below gum line) cleaning, where disease actually lives — it is cosmetic only and can miss serious disease</td></tr>
<tr><td class="myth-cell">Bad breath is just normal for pets</td><td class="fact-cell">Persistent bad breath is one of the most reliable early signs of dental disease, not a normal trait</td></tr>
<tr><td class="myth-cell">Dry food alone keeps teeth clean</td><td class="fact-cell">Dry food provides only mild mechanical cleaning; it does not replace brushing or professional scaling</td></tr>
</table>

<h2>The Dental Scaling Procedure</h2>
<p>Professional dental scaling at Shivraj Pet Clinic involves:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Pre-anaesthetic bloodwork</strong> — essential before general anaesthesia</li>
<li><strong>General anaesthesia</strong> — pets must be fully anaesthetised for thorough and safe scaling</li>
<li><strong>Scaling</strong> — ultrasonic and hand instruments remove tartar from all tooth surfaces</li>
<li><strong>Probing</strong> — checking for periodontal pockets and loose teeth</li>
<li><strong>Extraction</strong> — severely diseased teeth are extracted rather than left to cause ongoing pain and infection</li>
<li><strong>Polishing</strong> — smooths enamel to slow future tartar accumulation</li>
</ol>

<div class="post-warning">⚠️ Contact us promptly if you notice facial swelling, sudden refusal to eat, or heavy bleeding from the gums — these can indicate an advanced tooth root abscess needing urgent attention.</div>

<h2>Maintaining Oral Health at Home</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Tooth brushing</strong> — daily brushing with pet-safe toothpaste is the gold standard. Start slowly, let your pet lick the toothpaste first, introduce the brush gradually</li>
<li><strong>Dental chews</strong> — those with the VOHC (Veterinary Oral Health Council) seal have evidence behind them</li>
<li><strong>Dental diets</strong> — certain prescription diets help reduce tartar mechanically</li>
<li><strong>Water additives</strong> — some products reduce bacterial counts in drinking water</li>
</ul>
<p>Professional dental scaling is typically needed every 1–3 years depending on your pet's breed and oral hygiene routine. Small breeds (Shih Tzu, Dachshund, Pomeranians) tend to develop tartar faster and may need annual scaling.</p>

<h2>Frequently Asked Questions</h2>
<p><strong>Is dental scaling safe for older pets?</strong><br>Yes, with proper pre-anaesthetic screening. Age alone is not a reason to avoid necessary dental care — untreated dental disease is often the greater risk.</p>
<p><strong>How long does the procedure take?</strong><br>Typically 30–60 minutes depending on the extent of tartar and whether extractions are needed.</p>
<p><strong>Will my pet need to stay overnight?</strong><br>Most pets go home the same day, once fully recovered from anaesthesia.</p>
<p><strong>How can I tell if my pet is in dental pain?</strong><br>Pets rarely show obvious pain — watch for subtle signs like reduced chewing on one side, reluctance to play with toys, or reduced interest in hard treats.</p>
<p><strong>Do cats need dental scaling as much as dogs?</strong><br>Yes — cats develop dental disease at similarly high rates and often show fewer obvious signs, making regular checks important.</p>
<p><strong>Can I prevent the need for scaling with brushing alone?</strong><br>Regular brushing significantly reduces tartar buildup and can extend the time between professional scalings, but it does not eliminate the need entirely.</p>
<p><strong>What happens if dental disease is left untreated?</strong><br>Progressive tooth loss, chronic pain, and increased risk of the bacteria affecting the heart, kidneys, and liver.</p>
<p><strong>Is anaesthesia safe for my pet?</strong><br>We use modern anaesthesia protocols with pre-anaesthetic bloodwork and continuous monitoring, the same standard used in human hospitals.</p>
