<h2>What Is Acral Lick Dermatitis?</h2>
<p>Acral lick dermatitis develops when a dog repeatedly licks one localised area — almost always the front paw or lower leg — until the constant moisture and trauma cause inflammation, hair loss, and a thickened, raised patch of skin called a granuloma. What starts as a habit becomes self-perpetuating: the licking itself irritates the skin further, which triggers more licking.</p>

<h2>Common Causes</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Allergies</strong> — environmental or food allergies causing localised itching</li>
<li><strong>Anxiety or boredom</strong> — a compulsive behaviour similar to nail-biting in humans, often seen in dogs left alone for long hours</li>
<li><strong>Underlying pain</strong> — arthritis or an old joint injury can cause a dog to lick the area over the joint</li>
<li><strong>Parasites</strong> — fleas or mites causing localised irritation</li>
<li><strong>Skin or deep tissue infection</strong> — bacterial infection can both cause and result from chronic licking</li>
</ul>

<div class="post-highlight">🔍 Clinical insight: At Shivraj Pet Clinic, we frequently see lick granulomas in dogs that appear otherwise perfectly healthy — the underlying trigger is often behavioural (boredom or separation anxiety) rather than medical. Identifying which category your dog falls into changes the entire treatment approach.</div>

<h2>Signs to Watch For</h2>
<ul>
<li>Hair loss over one specific spot, usually the front paw or wrist</li>
<li>Red, inflamed, or raised thickened skin</li>
<li>Constant or repetitive licking at the same site</li>
<li>Open sores or wet, weeping wounds in advanced cases</li>
<li>The dog resists you touching the area</li>
</ul>

<h2>Treatment</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Identify the underlying cause</strong> — allergy testing, joint assessment, or a behavioural history, depending on the case</li>
<li><strong>Medication</strong> — antibiotics if infection is present; anti-inflammatories or anti-anxiety medication where appropriate</li>
<li><strong>E-collar or protective wrap</strong> — to break the physical licking cycle while the skin heals</li>
<li><strong>Behavioural enrichment</strong> — increased exercise, puzzle feeders, and structured attention for boredom-driven cases</li>
<li><strong>Allergy management</strong> — dietary trial or environmental control if allergy is the trigger</li>
</ul>

<div class="post-warning">⚠️ Contact a veterinarian immediately if you notice: an open wound that isn't healing, a foul smell from the site, visible tendon or bone, or the dog becoming lethargic or feverish. These suggest a deep infection that needs prompt treatment.</div>

<h2>Prevention</h2>
<p>Daily exercise, mental stimulation (puzzle toys, training sessions), consistent parasite prevention, and regular veterinary check-ups all reduce the risk of lick granulomas forming in the first place. If your dog has a history of anxiety, addressing it early — before a lick spot forms — is far easier than treating an established one.</p>

<h2>Frequently Asked Questions</h2>
<p><strong>Can lick granulomas heal completely?</strong><br>Yes, with consistent treatment of the underlying cause. Chronic, long-standing cases take longer and may leave a permanent bald patch or scar.</p>
<p><strong>Is this condition painful?</strong><br>Yes — the thickened skin and any underlying infection are uncomfortable, which is part of why dogs continue to lick.</p>
<p><strong>Which breeds are most prone to lick granulomas?</strong><br>Large, energetic breeds such as Labradors, Great Danes, and German Shepherds are commonly affected, often linked to under-stimulation.</p>
<p><strong>How long does treatment usually take?</strong><br>Mild cases can improve in 2–3 weeks. Chronic, deep granulomas may take months of consistent management.</p>
<p><strong>Should I stop my dog from licking immediately?</strong><br>Yes — using an E-collar or bandage to interrupt the cycle while you address the root cause speeds healing significantly.</p>
<p><strong>Can food allergies really cause a dog to lick its paw?</strong><br>Yes. Paw licking is one of the most common signs of food or environmental allergy in dogs.</p>
<p><strong>Will surgery ever be needed?</strong><br>Rarely, and only for very severe, non-responsive cases with significant tissue damage. Most cases resolve with medical and behavioural management.</p>
<p><strong>Can this happen to cats too?</strong><br>Cats can develop similar over-grooming lesions, though acral lick dermatitis specifically is far more common in dogs.</p>
