<h2>Why Dental Problems Are So Common in Guinea Pigs</h2>
<p>Unlike humans, a guinea pig's molars and incisors never stop growing. Normally, a high-fibre diet wears them down evenly through constant chewing. When diet lacks enough coarse fibre, or teeth grow unevenly, sharp points and overgrowth can develop — often on the back molars, where it's not visible without a proper oral exam.</p>

<h2>Common Causes</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Low-fibre diet</strong> — too many pellets and treats, not enough hay</li>
<li><strong>Genetic predisposition</strong> — some guinea pigs are simply more prone to malocclusion</li>
<li><strong>Insufficient wear</strong> — not enough coarse material to chew regularly</li>
<li><strong>Untreated minor misalignment</strong> — small issues that worsen gradually if not corrected</li>
</ul>

<h2>Warning Signs to Watch For</h2>
<ul>
<li>Drooling or a consistently wet chin</li>
<li>Dropping food while attempting to eat, or chewing on one side only</li>
<li>Weight loss despite food being available</li>
<li>Reduced or no interest in hard foods or hay</li>
<li>Facial swelling near the jawline</li>
<li>Reduced grooming, leading to a scruffy coat</li>
</ul>

<div class="post-warning">⚠️ A guinea pig that stops eating for more than a few hours needs urgent attention — like rabbits, guinea pigs can decline quickly once food intake drops, regardless of the underlying cause.</div>

<h2>What Happens at the Clinic</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Oral examination</strong> — back molars often require sedation to examine properly, since they're not visible on a casual look</li>
<li><strong>Dental correction</strong> — filing or trimming overgrown or sharp points under appropriate sedation</li>
<li><strong>Pain management</strong> — dental pain is often significant and addressed alongside the correction itself</li>
<li><strong>Nutritional support</strong> — syringe feeding where needed while the mouth heals</li>
<li><strong>Follow-up monitoring</strong> — since guinea pig teeth keep growing, some cases need periodic rechecks</li>
</ul>

<h2>Prevention</h2>
<ul>
<li>Provide unlimited access to good-quality grass hay as the majority of the diet</li>
<li>Limit pellets and treats to the recommended amount rather than free-feeding them</li>
<li>Offer safe chew items to encourage natural wear</li>
<li>Book a dental check if you notice any change in eating pattern, even a subtle one</li>
<li>Weigh your guinea pig regularly — gradual weight loss is often the earliest sign of a developing problem</li>
</ul>

<h2>Frequently Asked Questions</h2>
<p><strong>Can I check my guinea pig's back teeth myself at home?</strong><br>Not reliably — the molars sit far back and require proper equipment and often sedation to examine safely and thoroughly.</p>
<p><strong>Will my guinea pig need repeat dental treatments?</strong><br>Some guinea pigs, especially those with a genetic predisposition to malocclusion, need periodic dental rechecks throughout life.</p>
<p><strong>My guinea pig is still eating pellets but avoiding hay — is that a concern?</strong><br>Yes — preferring soft food over hay is a common early sign of dental discomfort and is worth checking before it progresses.</p>
<p><strong>Is dental treatment safe for guinea pigs, given they need sedation?</strong><br>Yes, with appropriate pre-anaesthetic assessment, sedation for dental work is a routine and safe procedure for guinea pigs.</p>
<p><strong>How much hay does my guinea pig actually need?</strong><br>As a general guide, hay should make up the majority of daily intake, roughly an amount equal to your guinea pig's own body size, available at all times.</p>
