<p>Not every symptom needs an emergency visit, and not every “he seems fine” is actually fine. The hardest part of pet ownership is telling the two apart in the moment. Here's a clear-eyed checklist.</p>

<div class="post-highlight">💡 Treat these as immediate emergencies: difficulty breathing, collapse or inability to stand, a bloated or painful abdomen (especially in deep-chested breeds), repeated vomiting or seizures, suspected poisoning, severe bleeding, or an inability to urinate. None of these should wait for a “morning appointment.”</div>

<h2>Watch Closely, Call If It Continues</h2>
<p>A single episode of vomiting in an otherwise bright, active dog, mild limping without swelling, or one instance of loose stool can often be monitored for a few hours — but persistence, worsening, or a combination of symptoms changes that calculus fast.</p>

<h2>The Combination Rule</h2>
<p>A single mild symptom is usually less urgent than the same symptom appearing alongside others — lethargy plus vomiting plus reduced appetite together is a stronger signal than any one of those alone.</p>

<h2>When in Doubt</h2>
<p>Call the clinic and describe what you're seeing. A quick phone conversation costs nothing and can tell you in two minutes whether this is a home-monitoring situation or a “come in now” one.</p>

<h2>Frequently Asked Questions</h2>
<p><strong>Is heavy panting always an emergency?</strong><br>Not always — panting from heat or exertion is normal, but panting combined with distress, blue-tinged gums, or an inability to settle is not.</p>
<p><strong>My dog seems fine but ate something he shouldn't have — do I wait for symptoms?</strong><br>No — call your vet immediately. Waiting for symptoms means treatment starts later, which matters for many toxins.</p>
<p><strong>What's a bloated abdomen a sign of?</strong><br>It can indicate gastric dilatation-volvulus (bloat), a life-threatening emergency especially in large, deep-chested breeds — this needs immediate veterinary attention, not observation.</p>
