<p>A seizure in your dog looks alarming, but what you do in the first few minutes matters more than panic. Here's a clear, practical guide.</p>

<h2>During the Seizure</h2>
<p>Stay calm and keep your hands away from your dog's mouth — dogs don't swallow their tongues, and you risk being accidentally bitten. Clear the area of furniture or objects your dog could hit. Time the seizure if you can; duration is one of the most useful pieces of information for your vet.</p>

<h2>After the Seizure</h2>
<p>Dogs often experience a disoriented recovery period afterward — confusion, temporary blindness, or unsteady walking. Keep your dog in a quiet, dim space and avoid crowding them until they're fully oriented.</p>

<h2>When It's an Emergency</h2>
<p>A seizure lasting more than a few minutes, multiple seizures in a row without full recovery between them (cluster seizures), or a first-ever seizure in an adult or senior dog all require immediate veterinary attention.</p>

<h2>Why the Cause Matters</h2>
<p>Seizures can stem from epilepsy, toxin exposure, low blood sugar, liver disease, or brain conditions. A single seizure that resolves quickly still warrants a veterinary conversation, since identifying an underlying cause changes the management plan significantly.</p>

<h2>Frequently Asked Questions</h2>
<p><strong>Should I put something in my dog's mouth during a seizure?</strong><br>No — this is unnecessary and risks injury to both you and your dog.</p>
<p><strong>How long is a seizure typically expected to last?</strong><br>Most seizures last under two minutes; anything approaching or exceeding five minutes is an emergency.</p>
<p><strong>Can seizures happen just once and never again?</strong><br>Yes, but any first-time seizure should still be evaluated to rule out an underlying, treatable cause.</p>
<p><strong>Is epilepsy the most common cause?</strong><br>It's one of several possible causes and is generally considered after other causes like toxin exposure or metabolic issues are ruled out.</p>
