<p>A vet who's excellent with dogs and cats isn't automatically equipped to treat a turtle, a parrot, or a rabbit. Exotic species have different anatomy, different normal values, different drug sensitivities, and different disease patterns — and general veterinary training doesn't cover all of it in depth.</p>

<h2>Why Exotic Anatomy Changes Everything</h2>
<p>A bird's respiratory system, a rabbit's digestive tract, and a reptile's metabolism don't map onto dog-and-cat medicine. Normal blood values, safe medication doses, and even how you physically restrain the animal for examination differ by species — mistakes here aren't just ineffective, they can be dangerous.</p>

<h2>The Risk of a Generalist-Only Clinic</h2>
<p>Exotic species are experts at hiding illness, which means by the time visible symptoms appear, the disease is often advanced. A vet without specific exotic training may miss early signs that an experienced exotic practitioner would catch immediately.</p>

<h2>What to Look For in an Exotic Vet</h2>
<p>Ask directly: does the clinic regularly see birds, rabbits, and reptiles, or occasionally? Do they have species-appropriate diagnostic tools and housing for exotic patients? Experience volume matters as much as willingness.</p>

<p><strong>Is exotic pet care more expensive than dog/cat care?</strong><br>It varies by clinic and case, but proper exotic care often requires specialized handling and diagnostics — ask your clinic for upfront cost clarity.</p>
