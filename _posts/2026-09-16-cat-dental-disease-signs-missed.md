<p>Cats are particularly good at hiding dental pain, which means dental disease often goes unnoticed longer in cats than in dogs.</p>

<h2>Subtle Signs</h2>
<p>Reduced interest in hard food, dropping food while eating, subtle drooling, and reduced grooming can all indicate dental discomfort.</p>

<h2>More Obvious Signs</h2>
<p>Visible tartar, red or bleeding gums, and noticeable bad breath are clearer signs when present.</p>

<h2>Why Regular Checks Matter</h2>
<p>Given how well cats mask dental pain, routine dental checks during wellness visits catch disease that owners might otherwise miss entirely.</p>

<h2>Frequently Asked Questions</h2>
<p><strong>**Do cats need their teeth brushed like dogs?**</strong><br>Yes, it's beneficial though often harder to establish — ask your vet for practical tips.</p>
<p><strong>**Is reduced grooming really linked to dental pain?**</strong><br>Yes, mouth discomfort can reduce a cat's willingness to groom normally.</p>
<p><strong>**How often should cats have dental checks?**</strong><br>Typically as part of regular wellness visits, at least annually.</p>
