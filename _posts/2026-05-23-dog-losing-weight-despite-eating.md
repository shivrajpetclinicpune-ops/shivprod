<p>A dog eating normally but still losing weight is a genuinely confusing symptom for owners — the intuitive logic of "eating fine means healthy" doesn't hold here, and that gap is exactly why it needs investigation.</p>

<h2>Why This Combination Is a Red Flag</h2>
<p>Weight loss despite normal or even increased appetite suggests the body isn't properly absorbing or using nutrients — rather than a simple case of not eating enough.</p>

<h2>Possible Causes</h2>
<p>Intestinal parasites, malabsorption disorders, diabetes, hyperthyroidism (less common in dogs than cats but possible), chronic organ disease, and certain cancers can all present this way.</p>

<h2>What to Track and Report</h2>
<p>Note how long the weight loss has been happening, whether appetite has actually increased, decreased, or stayed the same, and any other changes — energy level, thirst, stool consistency, coat quality. This history significantly speeds up diagnosis.</p>

<h2>Diagnosis Approach</h2>
<p>A physical exam and weight history are the starting point, typically followed by bloodwork, fecal testing for parasites, and imaging if a structural cause is suspected.</p>

<h2>Frequently Asked Questions</h2>
<p><strong>How much weight loss is significant?</strong><br>Any noticeable, unintentional weight loss over weeks is worth investigating rather than waiting to see if it continues.</p>
<p><strong>Could this be from increased activity or a diet change?</strong><br>It's possible, but if the dog's routine and diet haven't changed and weight loss continues, an underlying cause should be ruled out.</p>
<p><strong>Is this more common in older dogs?</strong><br>Some causes — chronic organ disease, certain cancers — are more common in seniors, but the symptom warrants evaluation at any age.</p>
<p><strong>Can parasites really cause significant weight loss?</strong><br>Yes, particularly in cases of heavy parasite burden, which is why fecal testing is a standard part of the workup.</p>
