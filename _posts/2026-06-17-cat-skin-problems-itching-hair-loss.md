Skin problems in cats can look very similar to one another: scabs, bald patches, over-grooming and scratching. The cause, however, varies widely, and so does the treatment. A quick look under the microscope often gives the answer during the visit.

## What you might see

- **Small crusty scabs** along the back and neck (vets call this "miliary dermatitis")
- **Bald patches** on the belly or inner thighs from over-licking. These are often mistaken for "hair falling out"
- **Round, scaly bald patches** on the face, ears or paws (classic ringworm)
- **Dark, coffee-ground wax** in the ears, and head shaking
- **Scratching around the head and neck**, sometimes with open sores
- **Red, raised sores** on the lip or belly
- **Black specks** in the fur (flea dirt). On a damp white tissue they dissolve into red-brown smudges

## Common causes

**1. Fleas and flea allergy:** the top cause. Even one or two flea bites can trigger intense itching in an allergic cat, and you may never see a live flea. Indoor cats in apartments get fleas too, carried in on clothes or by other pets.

**2. Ringworm (dermatophytosis):** a fungal infection, not a worm. Very common in kittens from shelters, rescues and pet shops. **It can spread to people**, especially children, as red, ring-shaped itchy patches. Treatment takes weeks and includes cleaning the home.

**3. Ear mites:** common in kittens. They cause dark ear wax and scratching that leads to sores behind the ears.

**4. Other mites:** *Notoedres* (feline scabies) causes intense itching and crusting on the head and ears. Less common mites include *Demodex* and *Cheyletiella*.

**5. Allergies:** food allergy (often to a protein such as chicken or fish) or environmental allergy (dust mites, pollen) causes itching, over-grooming and recurring sores.

**6. Stress-related over-grooming:** a cat may lick its belly bald after a house move, a new pet or a change in routine. Rule out other causes before blaming stress.

**7. Bacterial or yeast infection:** usually secondary to scratching.

## What we do at the clinic

Our in-house lab lets us check most of these during the visit:
- **Flea combing** and the wet-paper test for flea dirt.
- **Skin scraping and tape impressions** under the microscope for mites.
- **Ear cytology** for mites, yeast and bacteria.
- **Wood's lamp** screening and **hair plucks** under the microscope for ringworm. Fungal culture is sent out when needed.
- A **food elimination trial** plan, if food allergy is suspected. It takes 8 weeks of a strict special diet.

<div class="post-warning"><strong>Never use dog flea products on cats.</strong> Many dog spot-ons and sprays contain <strong>permethrin</strong>, which causes tremors, seizures and death in cats. Use only products labelled for cats, at the correct weight dose.</div>

## Treatment and home care

- **Treat all pets in the home** for fleas, every month, and wash bedding in hot water.
- **Ringworm:** medicated baths or dips, oral antifungal medicine, and **daily vacuuming and disinfecting**. Spores survive for months in the home.
- **Ear mites:** ear treatment for **all** cats and dogs in the house.
- **Allergies:** long-term control rather than a cure, with diet, flea control and anti-itch medicines.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can I catch my cat's skin problem?**\
Ringworm and some mites can affect people. Flea bites can cause itchy spots on ankles. Wash hands after handling, and see your doctor if you notice skin changes.

**Is coconut oil good for cat skin?**\
It may soothe dry skin a little, but it won't treat fleas, mites or ringworm, and cats will lick it off. Get a diagnosis first.

**How long does ringworm take to clear?**\
Usually 6–10 weeks, or longer in long-haired cats. We confirm a cure with repeat tests, not just by looking.
