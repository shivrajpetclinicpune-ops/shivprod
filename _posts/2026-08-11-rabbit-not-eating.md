<h2>Why Rabbits Not Eating Is a Genuine Emergency</h2>
<p>Rabbits have a digestive system that depends on constant movement. When food intake stops, gut motility can slow or halt entirely — a condition called <strong>GI stasis</strong>. Gas builds up, the gut environment shifts, and without prompt treatment this can become life-threatening within a day or two, not weeks.</p>
<p>This is different from a dog or cat skipping a meal out of stress or a mild stomach upset. In rabbits, the loss of appetite is very often a symptom of an underlying problem, not the problem itself — most commonly dental pain, an obstruction, or a painful condition elsewhere in the body.</p>

<h2>Common Causes</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Dental disease</strong> — overgrown or misaligned molars causing pain while chewing</li>
<li><strong>GI stasis</strong> — slowed or stopped gut motility, often secondary to low-fibre diet, stress, or pain</li>
<li><strong>Obstruction</strong> — a blockage from ingested fur or foreign material</li>
<li><strong>Pain from another source</strong> — such as a urinary issue, arthritis, or an abscess</li>
<li><strong>Stress</strong> — a change in environment, a new cage-mate, or a frightening event</li>
</ul>

<h2>Warning Signs to Watch For</h2>
<ul>
<li>No food eaten for several hours, or dramatically reduced interest in favourite foods</li>
<li>Fewer or no droppings being produced</li>
<li>Hunched, tense posture with reluctance to move</li>
<li>Teeth grinding (a common sign of pain in rabbits)</li>
<li>A bloated or firm abdomen</li>
<li>Lethargy or hiding more than usual</li>
</ul>

<div class="post-warning">⚠️ If your rabbit hasn't eaten or produced droppings in 12 hours or more, treat it as an emergency and contact a veterinarian immediately. Do not wait to "see if it improves" — early intervention makes a major difference in outcome.</div>

<h2>What Happens at the Clinic</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Physical examination</strong> — including a full dental check, since molar problems are a leading cause</li>
<li><strong>Pain assessment and management</strong> — rabbits mask pain well, so this is central to treatment</li>
<li><strong>Fluid therapy</strong> — to rehydrate and help restart gut motility</li>
<li><strong>Gut-motility medication</strong> — where appropriate, once obstruction has been ruled out</li>
<li><strong>Imaging</strong> — X-rays to check for obstruction or excessive gas build-up when indicated</li>
</ul>

<h2>Prevention</h2>
<ul>
<li>Feed a diet that's primarily good-quality grass hay, supplemented with fresh leafy greens</li>
<li>Keep fresh water available at all times</li>
<li>Book regular dental checks, since overgrown molars often cause no obvious external signs until eating stops</li>
<li>Minimise sudden changes to diet, housing, or companions where possible</li>
<li>Monitor daily food intake and droppings — catching a change early is the biggest factor in a good outcome</li>
</ul>

<h2>Frequently Asked Questions</h2>
<p><strong>My rabbit ate a little this morning but has stopped since — is that still an emergency?</strong><br>Yes. A significant drop in intake, even if not complete refusal, warrants a same-day check, especially if droppings have also reduced.</p>
<p><strong>Can I just syringe-feed my rabbit at home until it improves?</strong><br>Supportive feeding can help once a vet has assessed the cause, but doing it alone without knowing why your rabbit stopped eating can delay treatment of the underlying problem.</p>
<p><strong>Is GI stasis the same as a hairball blockage?</strong><br>Not always — stasis is a slowdown of gut movement that can happen with or without a physical blockage. Only an exam (and sometimes imaging) can tell the difference.</p>
<p><strong>How quickly can rabbits decline once they stop eating?</strong><br>Deterioration can happen within 24-48 hours, which is why same-day veterinary attention matters so much with this species.</p>
<p><strong>Do you see rabbit dental problems often?</strong><br>Yes — dental disease is one of the most common underlying causes of appetite loss we diagnose in pet rabbits.</p>
<p><strong>What should I bring to the appointment?</strong><br>Note roughly when your rabbit last ate and last produced droppings, and bring your rabbit in its normal carrier to minimise added stress.</p>
