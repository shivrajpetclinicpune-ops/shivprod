<p>Urinary symptoms in dogs span a wide range of urgency — from a manageable infection to a blockage that can become fatal within a day. Here's how to read the signs.</p>

<h2>Signs of a Urinary Tract Infection</h2>
<p>Frequent urination in small amounts, straining, blood-tinged urine, and accidents in a previously house-trained dog are common UTI signs. Uncomfortable but generally not immediately life-threatening, a UTI still needs treatment to prevent it from ascending to the kidneys.</p>

<h2>Bladder Stones</h2>
<p>Stones can cause similar straining and blood in urine, sometimes progressing to a partial or complete blockage. Some stones are visible on X-ray, while others require ultrasound for detection.</p>

<h2>Urinary Blockage — a True Emergency</h2>
<p>A dog straining repeatedly and producing little or no urine, especially a male dog, may have a urinary obstruction. This is a life-threatening emergency — a blocked bladder can lead to kidney damage and dangerous toxin buildup within hours.</p>

<h2>When to Go In Immediately</h2>
<p>Straining with no urine output, visible distress, a swollen abdomen, or vomiting alongside urinary straining all mean immediate veterinary attention, not a next-day appointment.</p>

<h2>Frequently Asked Questions</h2>
<p><strong>How do I tell a UTI apart from a blockage at home?</strong><br>You often can't reliably — both cause straining. The key differentiator is whether urine is actually being produced. If not, treat it as an emergency.</p>
<p><strong>Are male dogs at higher blockage risk than females?</strong><br>Yes, due to a narrower urethra, though blockage can occur in females too.</p>
<p><strong>Can diet affect stone formation?</strong><br>Yes — certain diets can influence the risk and type of stones formed; your vet can recommend a preventive diet after diagnosis.</p>
<p><strong>How is a blockage treated?</strong><br>Treatment typically involves relieving the obstruction (often via catheterization) and addressing any resulting metabolic complications — this requires immediate, in-clinic care.</p>
