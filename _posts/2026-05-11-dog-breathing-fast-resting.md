<p>Panting after a walk is normal. A dog breathing rapidly while lying still, relaxed, in a cool room, is not — and that distinction is the key to reading this symptom correctly.</p>

<h2>What's Normal</h2>
<p>A resting adult dog typically breathes at a slow, steady rate. Panting immediately after exercise, excitement, or in hot weather is expected and should settle within minutes once your dog cools down and calms down.</p>

<h2>What's Not Normal</h2>
<p>Rapid breathing at rest, with no obvious trigger, that persists for more than a few minutes is worth paying attention to — especially if it's paired with reluctance to lie down normally, blue-tinged gums or tongue, coughing, or visible effort with each breath.</p>

<h2>Possible Causes</h2>
<p>Heart disease, pain (including abdominal pain), fever, anemia, and primary respiratory or chest problems can all cause increased resting respiratory rate. The range of causes is wide enough that a home guess is unreliable — this is a symptom that benefits from an actual exam.</p>

<h2>When It's an Emergency</h2>
<p>Open-mouth breathing in a dog that doesn't normally pant, blue or pale gums, or breathing that looks labored (belly moving exaggeratedly with each breath) all mean immediate veterinary attention, not a wait-and-see period.</p>

<h2>Frequently Asked Questions</h2>
<p><strong>How do I count my dog's resting respiratory rate?</strong><br>Count chest rises for 30 seconds while your dog is calm and resting, then double it. Most healthy dogs fall in a normal range your vet can tell you for your dog's size — a rate that's climbing over time is more telling than a single count.</p>
<p><strong>Can anxiety cause rapid breathing at rest?</strong><br>Yes — situational stress can cause temporary rapid breathing, but it should settle once the trigger passes. Persistent rapid breathing with no clear trigger needs evaluation.</p>
<p><strong>Is this more concerning in older dogs?</strong><br>Yes — older dogs are more prone to heart and respiratory conditions that cause this symptom, so it should be taken seriously at any age but especially in seniors.</p>
<p><strong>Should I take my dog's temperature at home?</strong><br>If you have a proper pet thermometer, it can help, but don't delay a vet visit trying to gather more information first if breathing looks labored.</p>
