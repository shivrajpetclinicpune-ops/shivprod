<p>Most poisoning conversations focus on food — chocolate, onions, grapes. But some of the most dangerous exposures in Indian homes have nothing to do with the kitchen: rat poison left out for pest control, ordinary houseplants, and cleaning products under the sink. These are the poisons that catch pet parents off guard because they don't look like “pet hazards” at all.</p>

<div class="post-highlight">💡 Rodenticide (rat poison) is one of the most serious accidental poisonings in dogs and cats. Some formulations interfere with blood clotting — causing internal bleeding days later — which is exactly why it's dangerous even when your pet looks fine at first. If you know or suspect exposure, treat it as an emergency regardless of appearance.</div>

<h2>Rodenticide (Rat Poison)</h2>
<p>Common in homes and building common areas, different formulations cause different problems — some interfere with blood clotting, others cause severe neurological signs. Watch for lethargy, pale gums, unexplained bruising, difficulty breathing, or bleeding from the nose or gums, sometimes days after exposure.</p>

<h2>Toxic Houseplants</h2>
<p>Several common ornamental plants are toxic to dogs and cats, including lilies (especially dangerous to cats — even pollen contact can cause kidney failure), sago palm, oleander, and philodendron. Signs range from drooling and vomiting to, in severe cases, organ failure. If you're not sure whether a plant in your home is pet-safe, treat it as unsafe until confirmed otherwise.</p>

<h2>Household Chemicals</h2>
<p>Cleaning agents, disinfectants, drain cleaners, and pesticides stored within reach are a common cause of accidental exposure — usually through direct ingestion or licking a contaminated paw after walking through a freshly cleaned floor. Symptoms depend on the chemical but often include drooling, vomiting, mouth irritation, or breathing difficulty.</p>

<h2>What to Do If You Suspect Exposure</h2>
<p>Move your pet away from the source immediately. If you know what was ingested, keep the packaging — it tells your vet exactly what they're dealing with. Do not induce vomiting unless a vet tells you to; some chemicals cause more damage coming back up than staying down. Call your vet right away, even before symptoms appear.</p>

<h2>Frequently Asked Questions</h2>
<p><strong>My pet walked near rat poison but I didn't see them eat it — should I still worry?</strong><br>Yes. If exposure is possible, call your vet. The delayed-bleeding types of rodenticide are dangerous precisely because early signs are easy to miss.</p>
<p><strong>Are all lilies dangerous, or just some varieties?</strong><br>Most true lilies are considered highly toxic to cats specifically. If you have cats, it's safest to avoid lilies in the home entirely.</p>
<p><strong>My dog just licked a freshly mopped floor — is that a problem?</strong><br>Depends on the cleaning agent. Keep pets off freshly cleaned floors until fully dry, and check the product label for pet-safety information.</p>
<p><strong>Should I keep poison control information on hand?</strong><br>Yes — save your vet's emergency number where you can find it quickly. In a real exposure, minutes matter.</p>
