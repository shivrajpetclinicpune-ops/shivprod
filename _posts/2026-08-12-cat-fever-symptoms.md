<p>Cats hide fever even more effectively than dogs do, which means owners often only notice once the cat is clearly unwell — knowing the subtler signs helps catch it earlier.</p>

<h2>Signs of Fever in Cats</h2>
<p>Lethargy, reduced interest in food, hiding more than usual, warm ears, and a generally "off" demeanor are common signs. Cats often show fewer obvious behavioral changes than dogs, making close observation important.</p>

<h2>Confirming It Accurately</h2>
<p>A proper rectal temperature reading with a pet-appropriate thermometer is the only reliable way to confirm fever — visual or touch-based guessing isn't accurate enough in cats.</p>

<h2>Common Causes</h2>
<p>Infections, inflammation, abscesses (common after fights or bites), and less commonly immune-mediated conditions can all cause fever in cats.</p>

<h2>When to See a Vet</h2>
<p>Fever lasting more than a day, a high reading, or fever combined with lethargy, appetite loss, or hiding behavior should prompt a veterinary visit. Never give human fever medication to a cat — cats are especially sensitive to several common human drugs, including paracetamol, which can be fatal.</p>

<h2>Frequently Asked Questions</h2>
<p><strong>Are cats more sensitive to human medications than dogs?</strong><br>Yes, significantly — several common human drugs that are risky for dogs are outright dangerous or fatal for cats. Never medicate a cat without veterinary guidance.</p>
<p><strong>Can an abscess from a fight cause fever?</strong><br>Yes, this is a common cause, especially in cats with outdoor access — check for wounds if fever appears after a suspected fight.</p>
<p><strong>Is a warm nose a reliable sign in cats too?</strong><br>No, same as in dogs — nose temperature isn't a reliable fever indicator on its own.</p>
<p><strong>How is fever treated in cats?</strong><br>Treatment addresses the underlying cause; your vet will determine appropriate management based on diagnosis rather than treating the fever symptom alone.</p>
