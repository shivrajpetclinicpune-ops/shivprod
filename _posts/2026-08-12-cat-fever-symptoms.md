Fever is the body's response to infection or inflammation. In cats it often shows up as a **quiet, hiding cat who has stopped eating**, not as obvious shivering. Because cats hide illness so well, a fever is a clear signal that something needs attention.

## What is a normal temperature for a cat?

A healthy cat's rectal temperature is roughly **38.0–39.2 °C (100.5–102.5 °F)**. Mild stress, such as a car journey or a clinic visit, can push it a little higher.

- **Above 39.5 °C:** fever
- **Above 40.5 °C:** high fever, which needs prompt treatment
- **Below 37.5 °C** in a sick cat: a serious sign, which can mean shock

Warm ears, a dry nose or a warm belly are **not reliable** guides. The only accurate home method is a digital thermometer used rectally with a little petroleum jelly, held for about a minute. Many cats won't allow this. If yours struggles, stop and let us check it.

## Signs of fever in cats

- Lethargy, sleeping much more, hiding
- Not eating, or eating much less
- Not grooming, so the coat looks unkempt
- Faster breathing or a faster heart rate
- Shivering, or seeking warm places
- Sitting hunched with fur slightly raised

## Common causes

**Bite abscesses.** Very common in cats who go out, or who fight with strays from a society colony. A small puncture from another cat's tooth seals over, and an abscess forms under the skin within a few days. The cat runs a fever and may limp or have a soft, painful swelling. Abscesses need draining and antibiotics.

**Cat flu (upper respiratory infection).** Herpesvirus or calicivirus: sneezing, eye and nose discharge, mouth ulcers.

**Feline panleukopenia.** In unvaccinated kittens and young cats: fever (sometimes followed by a low temperature), vomiting, diarrhoea and a very low white cell count. This is an emergency.

**Other infections:** urinary or kidney infection, pyothorax (pus in the chest), uterine infection in unspayed females, blood parasites spread by fleas, and FIP (a persistent fever that doesn't respond to antibiotics).

**Inflammation:** pancreatitis, some cancers, and immune problems.

## What not to do

<div class="post-warning"><strong>Never give paracetamol (Crocin, Dolo, Calpol) to a cat.</strong> Cats can't break it down. A single tablet can destroy red blood cells and the liver and can kill. Aspirin, ibuprofen and nimesulide are also dangerous. Don't use leftover antibiotics either: they hide signs without treating the real cause.</div>

## When to come in

- Fever with not eating for **more than 24 hours** (sooner for kittens)
- Any fever in a **kitten**, especially with vomiting or diarrhoea
- A swelling, limp or wound after a fight
- Laboured or open-mouth breathing
- A fever that comes back after treatment

## What we do

We examine the cat thoroughly, check the temperature, and look for a source: wounds, mouth ulcers, the abdomen, and the chest sounds. A **CBC and biochemistry** in our in-house lab (about 15–20 minutes) show whether there's a bacterial infection, a virus like panleukopenia, or organ involvement. Abscesses are drained and flushed. Treatment then targets the cause, not just the temperature.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can I cool my cat down with a wet towel?**\
For fever from infection, don't try to cool your cat aggressively at home. Fever is part of fighting infection. Heatstroke is different: in that case, cool with room-temperature water and come straight in.

**Do kittens get fever after vaccination?**\
A mild temperature and sleepiness for a day can happen. Vomiting, swelling of the face, or breathing difficulty after a vaccine needs immediate attention.

**My cat's fever went down with an injection but she still isn't eating. Is that normal?**\
No. If appetite doesn't return, the underlying cause may still be active. Bring her back for a recheck.
