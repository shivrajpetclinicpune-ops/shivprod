<p>Sudden paralysis — a dog that can't move its hind legs, or suddenly drags a limb — is one of the clearest signals that something serious is happening internally, and time to treatment directly affects the outcome in several of the underlying conditions.</p>

<h2>Common Causes</h2>
<p>Intervertebral disc disease (a slipped or herniated disc pressing on the spinal cord) is one of the most common causes, particularly in long-backed breeds like Dachshunds. Other causes include spinal trauma, fibrocartilaginous embolism, tick-borne diseases, and, less commonly, tumors affecting the spine or nerves.</p>

<h2>Why Speed of Treatment Matters</h2>
<p>For several spinal conditions, the amount of time the spinal cord is compressed or without blood flow directly affects how much function can be recovered. This is a condition where "let's wait and see if it improves" can cost your dog mobility that could otherwise have been saved.</p>

<h2>What to Do</h2>
<p>Keep your dog as still as possible during transport to avoid further spinal injury, and get to a vet immediately. Do not attempt to force your dog to walk or stand.</p>

<h2>Diagnosis and Treatment</h2>
<p>Diagnosis often involves a neurological exam and imaging — X-rays can rule out some causes, while advanced imaging (arranged through referral when needed) may be required for a full picture of spinal cord involvement. Treatment ranges from strict rest and medication to surgery, depending on severity and cause.</p>

<h2>Frequently Asked Questions</h2>
<p><strong>Is this always a spinal problem?</strong><br>Not always, but spinal causes are among the most common and time-sensitive, which is why immediate evaluation is important regardless of the suspected cause.</p>
<p><strong>Which breeds are more prone to disc-related paralysis?</strong><br>Long-backed breeds like Dachshunds, along with Beagles and Cocker Spaniels, are at higher risk of intervertebral disc disease.</p>
<p><strong>Can partial paralysis improve on its own?</strong><br>Some mild cases improve with strict rest and medical management, but this should be a veterinary-directed decision, not a home judgment call — the risk of waiting too long is significant.</p>
<p><strong>What does the recovery process typically involve?</strong><br>Depending on the cause and severity, this can range from weeks of restricted activity to physical rehabilitation or surgical intervention.</p>
