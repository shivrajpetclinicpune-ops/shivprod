<p>"Cats vomit sometimes, that's normal" is true and also the exact belief that lets real problems go unnoticed for too long.</p>

<h2>What's Actually Normal</h2>
<p>An occasional hairball, especially in longer-haired cats, brought up once every week or two in an otherwise healthy, active cat is generally not concerning.</p>

<h2>What's Not Normal</h2>
<p>Vomiting more than once or twice a week, vomiting food (not just hair), weight loss alongside vomiting, or vomiting combined with lethargy or appetite changes all point toward something beyond routine hairballs.</p>

<h2>Possible Underlying Causes</h2>
<p>Chronic vomiting in cats can stem from dietary intolerance, inflammatory bowel disease, hyperthyroidism, kidney disease, or other chronic conditions — "just hairballs" is a diagnosis of exclusion, not a default assumption.</p>

<h2>When to See a Vet</h2>
<p>Frequency is the key signal — if you're seeing vomiting weekly or more, or if anything else has changed (appetite, weight, energy), it's time for an actual workup rather than continued at-home monitoring.</p>

<h2>Frequently Asked Questions</h2>
<p><strong>Does hairball paste prevent all vomiting-related issues?</strong><br>It can help with hair passage but won't address an underlying medical cause if one exists — don't rely on it if vomiting is frequent.</p>
<p><strong>Is vomiting after eating too fast a concern?</strong><br>Occasional regurgitation right after fast eating is usually mechanical, not medical — try smaller, slower-paced meals and monitor.</p>
<p><strong>Can chronic vomiting be linked to diet?</strong><br>Yes — food intolerance or sensitivity is a real and treatable cause worth investigating with your vet.</p>
<p><strong>What tests help find the cause of chronic vomiting?</strong><br>Bloodwork, and sometimes imaging or further gastrointestinal workup, depending on findings and history.</p>
