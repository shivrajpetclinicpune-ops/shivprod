<p>Blood in your cat's urine is never something to watch and wait on — the range of causes includes some of the most urgent conditions in feline medicine.</p>

<h2>Possible Causes</h2>
<p>Urinary tract infection, bladder inflammation (feline idiopathic cystitis), bladder stones, and urinary blockage can all cause blood in the urine, ranging from uncomfortable to immediately life-threatening.</p>

<h2>The Blockage Risk — Especially in Male Cats</h2>
<p>Male cats have a narrower urethra and are at higher risk of complete urinary blockage, where blood-tinged urine progresses to no urine output at all. This is one of the true feline emergencies — untreated, it can be fatal within a day or two.</p>

<h2>Warning Signs of Blockage</h2>
<p>Frequent trips to the litter box with little or no urine produced, straining, crying while attempting to urinate, and licking at the genital area are all signs that need immediate attention, not a wait-and-see approach.</p>

<h2>When to Go In Immediately</h2>
<p>Any male cat straining with no urine output should be treated as an emergency. Female cats with blood in urine should also be seen promptly, though blockage risk is lower.</p>

<h2>Frequently Asked Questions</h2>
<p><strong>Is this always a blockage?</strong><br>No, but it's serious enough that you can't tell the difference from home — a vet visit is needed to determine cause and rule out blockage.</p>
<p><strong>How quickly can blockage become fatal?</strong><br>It can become life-threatening within 24-48 hours of complete obstruction, which is why it's treated as a top-priority emergency.</p>
<p><strong>Are indoor male cats also at risk?</strong><br>Yes — this risk isn't related to outdoor access; it's anatomical and can affect any male cat.</p>
<p><strong>What does treatment for blockage involve?</strong><br>Typically relieving the obstruction (often via catheterization under sedation) and managing resulting complications — this requires immediate in-clinic care.</p>
