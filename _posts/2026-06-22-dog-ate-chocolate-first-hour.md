<p>The first hour after your dog eats chocolate is the window that matters most. Chocolate contains theobromine and caffeine, both of which dogs metabolize far more slowly than humans — and the danger depends heavily on the type of chocolate, the amount, and your dog's size. This is a step-by-step guide for that first hour, not a general <a href="toxic-foods-dogs-cats.html" class="internal-link">toxic-foods list</a>.</p>

<h2>Step 1: Identify What and How Much</h2>
<p>Check the wrapper. Dark chocolate, baking chocolate, and cocoa powder are far more dangerous than milk chocolate; white chocolate carries minimal risk. Estimate the amount eaten and your dog's weight — this is the single most useful piece of information you can give your vet on the phone.</p>

<h2>Step 2: Call Before You Act</h2>
<p>Do not induce vomiting at home without veterinary guidance — timing matters, and doing it incorrectly can cause more harm (aspiration, esophageal irritation) than benefit. Call your vet immediately with the chocolate type, quantity, and your dog's weight; they'll tell you whether this needs an in-clinic visit right now.</p>

<h2>Step 3: Watch for Early Signs</h2>
<p>Symptoms can take a few hours to appear and include restlessness, vomiting, diarrhea, increased thirst, a racing heart rate, and — in severe cases — tremors or seizures. Don't wait for symptoms to appear before calling; by the time signs show, absorption is already underway.</p>

<div class="post-highlight">💡 Depending on timing and dose, treatment may include inducing vomiting under clinical supervision, activated charcoal to limit absorption, and monitoring heart rate and hydration. If your vet recommends an in-person visit, don't delay for a “let's see how he does” period.</div>

<h2>What NOT to Do</h2>
<p>Don't give salt, hydrogen peroxide without vet guidance on dosing, or human anti-nausea medication. Don't assume “he seems fine” means he's in the clear — toxicity can take hours to become visible.</p>

<h2>Frequently Asked Questions</h2>
<p><strong>How much chocolate is actually dangerous?</strong><br>It depends on chocolate type and your dog's weight — dark and baking chocolate are dangerous in much smaller amounts than milk chocolate. There's no safe universal threshold; always call your vet with the specifics.</p>
<p><strong>My dog ate chocolate an hour ago and seems normal — are we in the clear?</strong><br>Not necessarily. Symptoms can be delayed. A normal-looking dog an hour in doesn't rule out toxicity — call anyway.</p>
<p><strong>Is white chocolate a concern?</strong><br>Minimal theobromine content means low risk, but large quantities can still cause an upset stomach from the fat and sugar content.</p>
<p><strong>What information should I have ready when I call?</strong><br>Type of chocolate, estimated quantity, your dog's weight, and how long ago it happened.</p>
